What is your advisor costing, and what would it have to earn back?

Enter your holdings and your advisor's fee schedule. The page compares your current setup with a low-cost two-fund index portfolio at the same stock/bond mix and the same assumed market return, so the only difference is cost. It shows what your numbers say; it does not tell you what to do.

To finish level with the index portfolio after {{ ending().years }} years, the advisor's work has to be worth about {{ percent(breakEven().rate) }} a year — roughly {{ money(breakEven().dollars) }} this year — in value you would not have captured on your own.

Things that can count toward it, and what limits each on your numbers:

  • Tax-loss harvesting — only works in taxable accounts. You have {{ money(byAccount().taxable) }} taxable ({{ percent(cost().balance ? byAccount().taxable / cost().balance : 0, 0) }} of the total); the IRA, Roth and 401(k) money ({{ money(byAccount().ira + byAccount().roth + byAccount()['401k']) }}) gets no benefit from it.
  • Asset location — holding bonds in tax-deferred accounts and stocks in taxable/Roth. A one-time setup, not a yearly effort.
  • Withdrawal sequencing and Roth conversions — mostly worth something near and in retirement.
  • Rebalancing — a once-a-year task with an index portfolio.
  • Behavioral coaching — keeping you invested through a crash. Real and potentially the biggest item, but it shows up in a few bad years rather than evenly every year.

Industry estimates (Vanguard's "Advisor's Alpha", Morningstar's "Gamma") put the combined value of these at up to roughly 1.5–3% a year in ideal conditions, much of it lumpy. A flat-fee or hourly fiduciary planner can deliver several of them at a fixed price while you hold the index funds yourself.
